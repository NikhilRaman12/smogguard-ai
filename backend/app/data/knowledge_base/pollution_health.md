# Understanding Air Pollution & Health Effects

## The Air Quality Index (AQI) Scale

The Air Quality Index (AQI) is a standardized measurement system used worldwide to communicate how polluted the air currently is or how polluted it is forecast to become. The AQI scale runs from 0 to 500, divided into six categories that correspond to different levels of health concern:

| AQI Range | Category | Health Implications |
|-----------|----------|-------------------|
| 0–50 | **Good** | Air quality is satisfactory. Air pollution poses little or no risk. |
| 51–100 | **Moderate** | Air quality is acceptable. Some pollutants may pose a moderate health concern for a very small number of people who are unusually sensitive. |
| 101–150 | **Unhealthy for Sensitive Groups** | Members of sensitive groups (children, elderly, those with respiratory conditions) may experience health effects. The general public is less likely to be affected. |
| 151–200 | **Unhealthy** | Some members of the general public may begin to experience health effects; members of sensitive groups may experience more serious effects. |
| 201–300 | **Very Unhealthy** | Health alert — the risk of health effects is increased for everyone. Outdoor activities should be significantly reduced. |
| 301–500 | **Hazardous** | Health warning of emergency conditions. The entire population is more likely to be affected. All outdoor exertion should be avoided. |

## PM2.5 — The Most Dangerous Pollutant

Fine Particulate Matter (PM2.5) refers to airborne particles with a diameter of 2.5 micrometres or less — roughly 30 times smaller than a human hair. These particles are small enough to penetrate deep into the lungs and even enter the bloodstream.

### Health Effects by Concentration Level

- **0–12 µg/m³ (Good):** Minimal risk. Normal outdoor activities are safe for everyone.
- **12.1–35.4 µg/m³ (Moderate):** Sensitive individuals may notice mild irritation. Long-term exposure at this level increases cardiovascular disease risk by approximately 6% per 10 µg/m³ increment.
- **35.5–55.4 µg/m³ (Unhealthy for Sensitive Groups):** People with asthma may experience increased symptoms. Children may develop reduced lung function with chronic exposure.
- **55.5–150.4 µg/m³ (Unhealthy):** Increased risk of respiratory infections, aggravated asthma, and irregular heartbeat. Studies show a 1.5% increase in daily mortality per 10 µg/m³ rise.
- **150.5–250.4 µg/m³ (Very Unhealthy):** Significant aggravation of heart and lung disease. Increased premature mortality risk. Visible haze typically present.
- **250.5+ µg/m³ (Hazardous):** Serious risk of premature death from cardiopulmonary disease. Emergency-level health conditions.

## Other Key Pollutants

### Nitrogen Dioxide (NO₂)
Primarily produced by vehicle emissions and power plants. Short-term exposure above 200 µg/m³ causes airway inflammation. Long-term exposure above 40 µg/m³ increases susceptibility to respiratory infections, particularly in children.

### Sulphur Dioxide (SO₂)
Released from burning fossil fuels containing sulphur, especially coal. Exposure above 350 µg/m³ (10-minute average) triggers bronchoconstriction in asthmatics. Contributes to acid rain and secondary PM2.5 formation.

### Ground-Level Ozone (O₃)
A secondary pollutant formed when nitrogen oxides and volatile organic compounds react in sunlight. Peaks during hot, sunny afternoons. Concentrations above 100 µg/m³ (8-hour average) reduce lung function, inflame airways, and aggravate asthma. Chronic exposure is linked to permanent lung damage.

### Carbon Monoxide (CO)
Produced by incomplete combustion in vehicles, generators, and indoor cooking. At elevated levels (above 10 mg/m³ over 8 hours), CO reduces the blood's oxygen-carrying capacity, causing headaches, dizziness, and — at very high concentrations — death.

## Vulnerable Populations

Certain groups face disproportionately higher risks from air pollution:

- **Children under 12:** Their lungs are still developing, they breathe more air per kilogram of body weight, and they spend more time outdoors. Chronic exposure can permanently reduce lung capacity by up to 20%.
- **Adults over 65:** Age-related decline in respiratory and cardiovascular function makes the elderly particularly susceptible. PM2.5 exposure increases stroke risk by 34% in this group.
- **Pregnant women:** Air pollution exposure during pregnancy is associated with low birth weight (risk increases by 10% per 10 µg/m³ PM2.5), preterm birth, and developmental delays.
- **People with asthma or COPD:** Pollutants trigger exacerbations, increase hospitalisation rates, and accelerate disease progression.
- **People with cardiovascular disease:** PM2.5 triggers systemic inflammation, promotes blood clotting, and destabilises arterial plaques, increasing heart attack and stroke risk.

## Winter Smog — The Science of Temperature Inversions

Winter smog is primarily caused by **temperature inversions** — a meteorological phenomenon that traps pollutants near the ground.

### How Temperature Inversions Work

Under normal conditions, air temperature decreases with altitude. Warm air near the ground rises, carrying pollutants upward where they disperse. During a temperature inversion, a layer of warm air sits above cooler surface air, acting as a lid that prevents vertical mixing.

### Why Inversions Are Common in Winter

1. **Radiative cooling:** Long winter nights allow the ground to radiate heat rapidly, cooling the air near the surface while air aloft remains warmer.
2. **Low sun angle:** Weak winter sunlight cannot warm the ground enough to break the inversion during the day.
3. **Calm winds:** High-pressure systems that bring cold, clear nights also bring calm winds, eliminating horizontal pollutant dispersion.
4. **Increased emissions:** Winter heating (wood, coal, gas) adds more pollutants precisely when dispersion is poorest.

### Indoor vs Outdoor PM2.5 Ratios

Without active filtration, indoor PM2.5 concentrations typically reach **50–70%** of outdoor levels in well-sealed buildings and **70–90%** in buildings with natural ventilation. During severe pollution events (AQI > 300), indoor concentrations can be nearly identical to outdoor concentrations unless HEPA filtration is used. A quality HEPA air purifier can reduce indoor PM2.5 by **80–95%**, making it the single most effective intervention for protecting indoor air quality.
